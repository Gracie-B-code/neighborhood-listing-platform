## 1. Value proposition (hypothesis)

**For neighborhood property professionals and local sponsors**, the Neighborhood Listing Platform is a proposed community hub for discovering local listings and sponsor information. The concept aims to make listing and sponsor content easier to find and manage while keeping published information accurate and accessible.

This is a product hypothesis, not a measured benefit. Discovery should establish:

- Which listing tasks property professionals actually perform, and what information must be accurate.
- How local sponsors want their business and sponsorship claims represented and approved.
- What residents need to find, and how they prefer to use the platform.
- Which outcomes stakeholders consider useful and how those outcomes could be measured without collecting unnecessary personal data.

**Current repository fact:** The home page presents cards for “Local Listings,” “Neighborhood Sponsors,” and “Voice Help.” It does not currently implement a listing workflow, sponsor approval, or voice assistance.

## 3. Discovery interview guide

Use only after instructor and participant approval. Ask open-ended follow-ups without presuming a problem or preferred solution. Do not request confidential business, resident, property, or personal information.

1. **Workflow:** Could you walk me through how you currently create or update information that residents need about local listings or services?
2. **Workflow:** What steps, if any, happen between preparing that information and making it available to residents?
3. **Audience:** Who do you expect to use this information, and what are they usually trying to find?
4. **Content approval:** How do you decide whether listing information is accurate and ready to publish?
5. **Content approval:** What review or approval, if any, should happen before a sponsor description or sponsorship claim is published?
6. **Success measures:** What would tell you that a neighborhood information service is useful? How do you assess that today?
7. **Accessibility:** Are there ways people need to access or contribute this information that current tools do not support?
8. **Priorities and risks:** If you could improve one part of this process, what would you choose, and what should a new service avoid?

Optional neutral probes: “Can you give an example without sharing identifying details?” “What happened next?” “Is there another perspective we should consider?”

## 7. Content approval workflow

Apply the same auditable status flow separately to listing content and sponsor claims:

1. **Draft:** Submitter prepares content; it is private and not publicly discoverable.
2. **Submitted:** Submitter confirms accuracy and authority to submit; the system records a version and submission time.
3. **In review:** An assigned reviewer checks completeness, accuracy, required authorization/evidence, and appropriate labeling.
4. **Returned:** Reviewer records a specific reason; submitter edits and resubmits. Returned content stays unpublished.
5. **Approved:** Reviewer records the decision, version, and time. Sponsor claims are approved only as submitted; approval must not imply independent verification beyond the actual review performed.
6. **Published:** Only an approved version is made public. Sponsor material is clearly labeled.
7. **Changed, expired, or withdrawn:** Material edits require a new review. Expired or withdrawn content is unpublished; retain only the minimum history needed for accountability.

**Unresolved policy questions:** Who may review or approve? What counts as a material change? What evidence is appropriate for a sponsor claim? How long should content and review history remain? Confirm these with stakeholders and the instructor before implementing policy.
